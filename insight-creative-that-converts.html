Creative that converts: a brief for the algorithm and the human

The best-performing creative does two jobs at once: it stops the scroll and gives the brain a reason to act. Most briefs only ask for one.

Adinfix Editorial 5 minute read 27 August 2026
03 / The creative brief Attention is the entry fee. Relevance is the sale.

Performance marketing has an uncomfortable truth at its centre: most ads are not rejected because the offer is bad. They are rejected because the first three seconds did not earn the next three. The creative failed before the product had a chance to speak.

That is why “make it perform” is not a useful brief. It describes the outcome without changing the inputs. A better brief asks two questions at once: what will make a human stop, and what will make that same human believe this is for them?

Two jobs, one asset

Creative in a performance context has to work on two levels. The first is mechanical: interrupt the scroll, create a pattern break, give the eye a reason to pause. The second is psychological: make the viewer feel seen, give them a clear next step, and reduce the risk of acting.

Most creative optimises for one and neglects the other. A flashy hook with no relevance gets attention and no clicks. A thoughtful product explanation with a weak opening never gets watched. The craft is in holding both.

“The hook is not the idea. The hook is the door. The idea is why anyone walks through it.”

What the brief should contain

A good creative brief is a thinking tool, not a form. Whether it is one page or ten, it should answer seven things:

  1. The business problem. What are we trying to move: pipeline, qualified leads, revenue, retention, share of search?
  2. The audience's internal monologue. What are they already saying to themselves about this problem? Use their words, not the category's.
  3. The tension. What is the gap between what they want and what they currently believe or do?
  4. The one thing. If they remember one sentence, what should it be?
  5. The proof. Why should they believe it? A number, a demo, a customer story, a demonstration, a guarantee.
  6. The next step. One action. Not three. Not “learn more.”
  7. The constraints. Platform, format, length, brand rules, legal requirements, production budget.

If the brief cannot fit on one side of A4, it is probably hiding a decision. Make the decision first.

Hook, proof, action

Most short-form performance creative can be understood in three movements: hook, proof, action. The proportions change by platform and audience, but the sequence rarely does.

The hook (0–3 seconds)

The hook should create a pattern break that is relevant to the audience. There are several reliable openings: a surprising claim, a direct callout, a visual contradiction, a specific number, a question the viewer is already asking. The common thread is specificity. “Are you tired of slow growth?” is not a hook. “Your best-performing post is probably the one you almost deleted” is.

Avoid the throat-clearing intro. Do not introduce the company, the founder or the product category before the viewer has a reason to care. Earn the next second first.

The proof (3–15 seconds)

Proof is where most ads lose the plot. They move from a strong hook to a generic benefit. Instead, show the evidence: the before/after, the demo, the customer's face, the number, the mechanism. The proof should feel like a natural answer to the question the hook raised.

For a service business, proof might be a client quote with a specific outcome. For a product, it might be a 3-second demonstration. For a B2B brand, it might be a chart, a process or a named case study. The format matters less than the specificity.

The action (last 5–8 seconds)

The action should be one clear next step, phrased in the language of value rather than the language of the interface. “Book a growth audit” is better than “Submit.” “See the 30-second teardown” is better than “Learn more.” If the viewer has to think about what happens next, the CTA is doing too little.

The one-page test

Write the hook, the proof and the action on three lines. If you cannot explain how the proof answers the hook, the concept is not ready for production. If the action does not feel like the natural next step, the offer or the audience is wrong.

Test the idea, not just the button

Most testing programmes spend their energy on micro-variations: button colour, headline length, image crop. Those tests have their place, but they rarely rescue a weak concept. The bigger gains come from testing the idea itself.

Test different tensions, different proof types, different openings. Give each concept enough budget and time to produce a signal. Judge it on the business measure, not just CTR. A high CTR with a low conversion rate can be a sign that the hook attracted the wrong audience.

Keep a learning log. Record what was tested, what happened and what it changed. The next brief should start with that log, not a blank page.

The takeaway

Creative that converts is not a magic trick. It is a disciplined answer to a real question, delivered with enough craft to earn attention and enough proof to earn belief. The algorithm rewards the same thing the human does: relevance, specificity and a clear next step.

Start with the tension. Write the one thing. Choose the proof. Then make it impossible to ignore.
